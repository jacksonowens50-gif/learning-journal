## 1. The prompt: ninety seconds on why it made that up

Written from memory, before rereading the one-pager.

> It isn't looking anything up. There's no database behind it and no step where
> it checks whether what it's saying is true.
>
> What it does is predict the next chunk of text, over and over. For each chunk
> it works out what's most likely to come next, given everything in front of it,
> and picks from that.
>
> Most of the time likely and true are the same thing, because most of what it
> learned from was written by people being accurate. When they come apart,
> nothing prefers the true one.
>
> So if I ask it for the Q3 travel variance and I haven't given it the file, it
> doesn't tell me it doesn't have the file. It gives me a number, in the right
> units, with the right sign, written exactly like every variance write-up it's
> ever seen. It isn't lying and it isn't broken. I asked what words plausibly
> come next, and those were the words.

The comparison that makes it land for a finance audience: **`VLOOKUP` either
finds the value or gives you `#N/A`. This never gives you `#N/A`.**

## 2. Probability of what, exactly

My first take was "there isn't a truth check, it's just taking what you put in
and giving you the best probability." Right, but the sharp version is the second
half: probability of *what*.

Not the probability that the answer is correct — it has no quantity for that.
The probability that this is **what comes next in text that looks like this**.

For the variance question it isn't computing "how likely is this the right
number." It's computing "how likely is a number, here, in this shape, in a
sentence that reads like a variance write-up." A number is overwhelmingly likely
in that slot. *Which* number is nearly incidental.

## 3. Why it doesn't just say "I don't know"

Because that sentence is rare in what it learned from. Variance write-ups almost
never contain it. Refusal is low-probability text, so it loses to a confident
figure, which is high-probability text.

It isn't being evasive. It's being ordinary.

## 4. Fluency is not evidence

The uncomfortable one, and the reason this fools capable people. In every other
professional setting a well-structured, confident, correctly formatted answer is
at least weak evidence that the person knows what they're talking about. That
heuristic has worked my entire career. This is the thing that defeats it.

The rule that fits on a sticky note: **never ask it for a number — give it the
numbers and ask it to work with them.**

## 5. It has no memory, and that's where the bill comes from

The context window holds everything at once: instructions, documents, the whole
conversation, and its own answer as it writes it. The model itself is stateless.
It doesn't recall my last message — the entire conversation is re-sent from the
top on every single turn.

**I'm not talking to an assistant who remembers me. I'm re-briefing a consultant
who's never met me, from scratch, before every question — and I pay for the
re-briefing each time.**

Two consequences, both commercial:

- Cost grows with conversation *length*, not question difficulty. Question twenty
  costs more than question one even if it's shorter.
- When the window fills, something gets dropped, and whatever the software drops
  is where "it forgot what I told it" comes from. That's a design decision
  somebody made, not a property of the model.

Also worth knowing: financial documents tokenize badly. `1,247,893` fragments
into several tokens, and so do account codes, dates and table formatting. A page
of a budget file costs more than a page of a memo. Page count is the wrong unit.

## 6. The three levers

| | Prompting | Retrieval (RAG) | Fine-tuning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Changes | The instructions | What's in front of it at question time | The model itself |
| Lead time | Hours | Weeks to months | Months, plus maintenance |
| Doesn't fix | It not knowing our data | A task it fundamentally can't do | Knowledge |

Try them in that order. Almost everything that arrives as "we need to train it on
our data" is prompting or retrieval.

**Fine-tuning teaches a shape, not a fact.** If a client wants the model to know
their catalogue, fine-tuning is wrong — the catalogue changes Tuesday and the
weights don't.

And the line I care about most:

**Retrieval is a data project, not an AI project.** Whether the right document
reaches the model is a question of where documents live, how current they are,
how they're structured and who's allowed to see them. No model fixes scattered,
undocumented, ungoverned data. That's Days 14 through 16 one layer up, and it's
the reason the data person belongs in the AI meeting instead of waiting down the
hall for the AI team to finish.

## 7. "Will it train on our data?"

Training happened once and produced fixed weights — hence the knowledge cutoff,
and hence it never knew anything about the client. Inference doesn't change them.
A call doesn't modify the model and the model retains nothing.

Contractually, Anthropic's commercial products (API, Claude for Work) don't use
inputs or outputs for training by default. Two caveats I want to keep:

- **The opt-in is one click.** Thumbs up/down shares that conversation, and
  feedback data can be retained up to five years. An admin can disable it
  org-wide, but it's on by default.
- **Consumer and commercial terms are different documents.** The free version
  staff are already using in a browser isn't governed by the enterprise
  agreement under procurement review. Most real exposure sits there.

The redirect worth making: training is rarely the question that matters. Where is
it logged, for how long, in which region, which subprocessors, who at the vendor
can read it — those are contractual, they vary by tier, and they're what counsel
should be asking about.

## 8. Five questions before approving an AI project

1. What does this do when it's wrong, and who finds out?
2. Where does the data it reads live today, and who's allowed to see it?
3. Cost per use × realistic volume, per month? Not the licence — the usage curve.
4. What's it replacing, and what does that cost today?
5. Who owns it in six months when the model version changes?

Number two is mine. Permissions have to survive retrieval — an assistant that
answers questions its user isn't cleared to ask is a compliance incident with a
chat interface on it. That's the row-level security section of
`auth-cheatsheet.md`, one layer up, and almost nobody proposing a pilot has
thought about it.
