# Day 14 — Tue Aug 4: Databases + CRUD from the inside

Built a database from scratch for the first time. Three tables, all four CRUD
operations, transactions, and one deliberate act of vandalism.

Deliverable: `mini-erp/orders_db.py` — becomes the foundation of tomorrow's
FastAPI app.

---

## 1. What I built

`customers`, `orders`, `order_items`. Five customers, eight orders, fifteen line
items. SQLite, which is just a file sitting in the folder — no server, no
install, no credentials. That's why it's a teaching tool and also why nobody runs
a company on it: one file means one machine.

Three tables and not two, because an order has *many* line items, so price and
quantity can't live on the order row without repeating. Taking the data apart
like that is **normalization**, and it's correct for recording orders. It is
also the reason reporting on them costs something, which turned out to be the
whole point of the day.

Things that earned their place:

- **`PRIMARY KEY`** — the database refuses a duplicate instead of accepting it
  and letting me find it in a reconciliation six months later.
- **`FOREIGN KEY`** — a row must point at something that exists. This is the
  orphan `sales_rep_id` I deliberately planted in my Day 8 JSON file. There,
  nothing stopped me. Here, something does.
- **`PRAGMA foreign_keys = ON`** — SQLite ignores foreign keys unless you turn
  them on, per connection. Without that line every constraint I wrote is
  decoration. The general lesson outlives SQLite: a constraint that's *written
  in the schema* and one that's *enforced* are different things. When a client
  says "the database enforces that," the follow-up is whether it does, or
  whether it's just written down.
- **`?` placeholders, never f-strings.** The famous reason is SQL injection. The
  reason that would actually have gotten me today is a customer named O'Brien —
  the apostrophe closes the string and the query dies. Tomorrow these values
  arrive from strangers over HTTP.

## 2. OLTP vs OLAP, without the jargon

Systems that **record** are built differently from systems that **analyze**.

That's the whole distinction. The acronyms explain nothing — "online transaction
processing" is a phrase from the 1970s that survived past its usefulness. Every
strange thing about a production database falls out of one design goal: write one
row, correctly, right now, while four hundred other people are also writing one
row.

I have spent my entire career on the analyze side of that line without knowing
the line existed.

## 3. Why not report off prod — for the CFO

The situation: their Tableau dashboards are timing out. Their data team says buy
a bigger server. No locks, no contention, no normalization in this section — those
are causes, and nobody funds a cause.

**The same database that takes your customers' orders is being asked to crunch a
year of history.** Checkout slows for real customers while your dashboard spins.
That's the sentence.

Four reasons, in escalating order:

**One — they share a budget.** The database can do a fixed amount of work per
second. My year-to-date query and your customer's checkout are spending from the
same account. The visible cost isn't a slow report, it's a slow *store*.

**Two — big reads can make writes wait.** Worse than sharing. To keep two people
from corrupting the same record, the database makes one of them wait. So a long
report doesn't just consume capacity, it can put orders in a queue behind it.
That's not a slow store anymore, that's an order that fails.

**Three — it's the wrong shape.** The production database is built for writing
one row, so the data is scattered across dozens of narrow tables on purpose. I
proved this today: "how much has each customer spent" — one line of business
English — required joining three tables back together. Every dashboard pays that
reassembly cost on every single refresh, forever.

**Four — the history isn't there.** This is the one I'd lead with now, and the
one I didn't have yesterday. The production database stores *what is true now*.
When an order goes from pending to shipped, the word "pending" is overwritten —
not archived, not versioned, gone. I watched it happen: one `UPDATE`, and the
previous state had never existed.

So "how long did orders take to ship last quarter, by region" isn't slow to
answer. **It's unanswerable.** Not at any budget, on any hardware. A bigger
server cannot buy back a value that was overwritten in March.

And a fifth I didn't expect to find in my own code: my revenue query had
`WHERE status != 'cancelled'` sitting inside it. That's a business rule living in
one file. Someone in another department writes their own query, doesn't exclude
cancelled, and brings a different revenue number to the same meeting. Neither of
us is wrong and neither of us can prove it. That's not a technical problem —
that's the reason organizations buy a place where "revenue" gets defined once.

## 4. The objection I'll actually get

*"We'll just point Tableau at a read replica."*

A replica is a live copy of production that absorbs the read traffic. It's a real
solution and it genuinely fixes reasons one and two. Saying so out loud is worth
more than pretending otherwise — it's the thing that makes the rest of the
recommendation believable.

It does nothing for three or four. Same shape, same missing history. A replica is
a copy of the problem with better manners.

## 5. So what I'd propose

A separate database for reporting, loaded on a schedule, shaped for reading, and
keeping the history production throws away.

Which is a data warehouse — and I got there by arguing from the failures rather
than by being told about it. That's Weeks 7 and 8 arrived at in one paragraph.

## 6. What I had yesterday and what I missed

Yesterday's guess, written blind: competition for resources, and tables shaped
for recording rather than summing.

I had reasons **one and three**. Both correct, both the ones you can reason your
way to from the outside.

I missed **two** entirely — I knew reports and transactions competed, but I
didn't know a read could actively *block* a write. And I missed **four**, which
is the strongest argument on the page, because it's the only one a bigger server
can't answer. If I'd walked into a client room yesterday I'd have made a decent
case that could be defeated by "we'll add a replica." Today it can't be.

Also worth recording: yesterday I noticed my last employer had already solved
this with extracts and I'd never registered it as an architecture decision. Now I
can name what the extract was actually buying — mostly one and two, partially
three, and nothing at all for four. Our history problem was still a history
problem.

## 7. The `WHERE`-less UPDATE, on purpose

Copied the database to `scratch.db` and ran `UPDATE orders SET status =
'shipped'` with no `WHERE`.

Eight rows changed, four milliseconds, no warning, no confirmation, success. That
is what it looks like. It isn't dramatic and it isn't hard to do, which is
exactly the problem — it's indistinguishable from working until someone calls.

The specific damage: order 105 had been **cancelled** and is now shipped, with no
record it was ever cancelled. A fulfillment system reading that column would ship
an order a customer called to cancel, and revenue quietly gained $480 because the
cancelled filter now excludes nothing.

Then `DELETE FROM order_items` with no `WHERE`. Fifteen rows gone, and the
revenue report returned **zero rows — it didn't error**. A dashboard on top of
that shows blank panels, and blank looks like "no sales yet," not like "someone
destroyed a table." Customers and orders were all still sitting there, so any
report that didn't touch money still looked healthy.

Silent, partial, plausible wrongness. That's the signature of data damage, and
it's why you hear about it from a person instead of an alert.

**The foreign keys did not save me.** They were on, they were real, and this was
still allowed — a foreign key stops *orphans*, so it would have blocked deleting
a customer who still had orders. Deleting the children is legal. Every order now
has zero line items, which is structurally valid and business-nonsense.
Constraints protect referential integrity, not sanity. Nothing in a database will
stop you from writing something coherent and wrong.

Three habits out of this:

1. Type the `WHERE` first, then go back and put `UPDATE` in front of it.
2. Run the identical `WHERE` as a `SELECT` before you run it as an `UPDATE`. 900
   rows when you expected one, and you found out for free.
3. Check `rowcount` in code and roll back if it isn't what you expected. Four
   lines:

```python
cur = conn.execute("UPDATE orders SET status = ? WHERE order_id = ?", (status, oid))
if cur.rowcount != 1:
    conn.rollback()
    raise ValueError(f"expected 1 row, changed {cur.rowcount}")
conn.commit()
```

Same instinct as `config.validate()` in `econ-report`: check the assumption at
the moment you make it, not four steps downstream when a number looks strange.

## 8. Transactions

Inserted a row on one connection and queried from a second. Predicted 6, 5, 6 and
got 6, 5, 6.

The correction I needed: the uncommitted row wasn't "there but not saved." It was
in one connection's **private draft**. Lose power at that moment and it never
existed — nothing to clean up, because nothing happened. That's what makes a
transaction a promise rather than a formality: *until you commit, your work is
real to you and doesn't exist to anyone else.*

Then the opposite: wrapped an insert in `with conn:` and raised an exception
inside the block. Before 6, after 6. Inserted and un-inserted with no trace.

The word to keep is **lock**. The database serializes writes so two people can't
corrupt the same bytes, and that waiting is the mechanism behind reason two
above. It's the difference between "the store is slow" and "the order failed."
